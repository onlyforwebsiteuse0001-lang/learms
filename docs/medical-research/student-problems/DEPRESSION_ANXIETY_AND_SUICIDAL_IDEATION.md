# Depression, anxiety and suicidal ideation in medical students

Safety note: this research file does not diagnose, screen, or provide crisis care. Suicidal thoughts require human, context-sensitive assessment and appropriate local emergency or clinical support.

## Topic
Global depression and suicidal-ideation estimates

### Source
Rotenstein et al. systematic review and meta-analysis of 195 studies involving 129,123 medical students in 47 countries. [MED-087]

### Key Finding
The crude summary prevalence was 27.2% (95% CI 24.7–29.9) for depression or depressive symptoms and 11.1% (95% CI 9.0–13.7) for suicidal ideation. Estimates varied substantially by instrument and recall period. Only 15.7% of students who screened positive for depression reportedly sought psychiatric treatment. A positive screen is not equivalent to a diagnosed depressive disorder.

### Relevance to Learms
Offer low-friction, private navigation to support; do not infer diagnosis from behavior, study performance, or a single questionnaire. Support discovery should not depend on declaring a diagnosis.

### Citation
Rotenstein LS, Ramos MA, Torre M, et al. (2016). Prevalence of Depression, Depressive Symptoms, and Suicidal Ideation Among Medical Students: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. *JAMA*, 316(21), 2214–2236. doi:10.1001/jama.2016.17324.

## Topic
Global anxiety estimate

### Source
Quek et al. meta-analysis of 69 studies and 40,348 medical students. [MED-088]

### Key Finding
The pooled prevalence of anxiety was 33.8% (95% CI 29.2–38.7), with substantial geographic and methodological variation. The source used study-level screening definitions and did not establish that medical school caused anxiety for each participant.

### Relevance to Learms
Do not present 33.8% as a Pakistan estimate or as an individual's baseline risk. Anxiety support content should remain distinct from exam-timing reminders that may themselves increase pressure.

### Citation
Quek TTC, Tam WWS, Tran BX, et al. (2019). The Global Prevalence of Anxiety Among Medical Students: A Meta-Analysis. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 16(15), 2735. doi:10.3390/ijerph16152735.

## Topic
Pakistan-specific depression evidence is heterogeneous

### Source
Ebrahimi et al. systematic review/meta-analysis of 71 Pakistan studies contributing 72 estimates and approximately 23,000 medical students. Search through September 2025. [MED-092]

### Key Finding
Reported depressive-symptom prevalence ranged from 9% to 94%; heterogeneity was I²=99.15%. Instruments and cut-offs varied, with DASS-21, PHQ-9, HADS and other measures represented. The authors stated that a single pooled estimate may not adequately summarize the findings and noted publication bias and methodological limitations.

### Relevance to Learms
No “Pakistan medical-student depression rate” should appear without the range, design and heterogeneity warning. A dashboard should compare methods, not rank institutions by incompatible screening results.

### Citation
Ebrahimi H et al. (2026). Prevalence of depressive symptoms among medical students in Pakistan: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *BMJ Open*, 16(6), e116544. doi:10.1136/bmjopen-2026-116544.

## Topic
A multisite Pakistan screening result is not a diagnosis

### Source
Jadoon et al. cross-sectional convenience sample of 437 students from six medical schools using the Aga Khan University Anxiety and Depression Scale. [MED-094]

### Key Finding
Symptoms above the study threshold were reported in 45.5% of respondents. Medical-school year, annual-examination failure, study hours, family history and prior depression were associated factors in the study. Convenience sampling, self-report and cross-sectional design prevent national prevalence or causal conclusions.

### Relevance to Learms
Capture assessment failure as a possible support trigger only when the learner asks for help; never label failure as evidence of mental illness.

### Citation
Jadoon NA, Yaqoob R, Raza A, Shehzad MA, Zeshan SC. (2010). Anxiety and depression among medical students: a cross-sectional study. *Journal of the Pakistan Medical Association*, 60(8), 699–702. PMID:20726214.

## Topic
Digital support for suicidal thoughts has a low-certainty boundary

### Source
WHO mhGAP evidence-centre recommendation on digital interventions for self-harm and suicide. [MED-104]

### Key Finding
WHO says stand-alone digital interventions based on CBT, DBT, problem-solving therapy or mindfulness should be considered as support for people with suicidal thoughts; the recommendation is **conditional** and the certainty of evidence is **low**. This is not evidence that an ordinary learning app can assess or manage acute risk.

### Relevance to Learms
Learms should not calculate a suicide-risk score, promise confidentiality it cannot guarantee, or substitute content for clinical assessment. At minimum, distress-related screens need immediate exit, verified human-support routing, non-judgmental language, and no streak/engagement pressure. Pakistan-specific crisis contacts suitable for product hard-coding were **not found and verified** in this pass.

### Citation
World Health Organization (2023). *Digital interventions: mhGAP Evidence Centre—self-harm and suicide*. https://www.who.int/teams/mental-health-and-substance-use/treatment-care/mental-health-gap-action-programme/evidence-centre/self-harm-and-suicide/digital-interventions (accessed 2026-09-30).
