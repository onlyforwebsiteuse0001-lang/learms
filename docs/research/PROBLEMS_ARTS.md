## 1. Family disapproval is the *primary* barrier — ahead of money

### Topic: Socio-cultural resistance to arts education in Pakistan
### Source: "Theatre: Foundation and Facilitator of other/all Media Arts", *Voyage Journal of Educational Studies* — interview study with students at the **School of Visual and Performing Arts (SOVAPA), Arts Council of Pakistan, Karachi** and **ZVMG Rangoonwala Community Centre**
### Key Finding **[MED — small qualitative interview study, but the only Pakistan-specific student-level evidence found for this field]**

The study reports **two dominant constraints**, in this order:

1. **Parental discomfort.** *"A segment of parents feel that the profession of art will not give a
   decent income to their child; they prefer engineering, medicine, business studies, and in the
   present times, computers and IT."* Children are "often discouraged from pursuing arts,
   humanities, and social sciences."
2. **Financial constraints** — and specifically that **art education costs *more* than other fields**
   because of "a continuous requirement of expenses incurred during the course duration"
   (materials, consumables) "compared to other simple, only theoretical educational fields."

Secondary findings from the interviews:
- 19 students reported **no** difficulty — parental support plus financial stability. So the barrier
  is class-stratified, not universal.
- 4 students were pursuing their field of interest but **worried about future financial security**.
- 4 students reported **difficulty finding information about art and design courses** — "they were
  not widely publicized and there was limited information."
- 2 were **undecided between Fine Arts and Design** — i.e. unadvised on the difference.
- Students from average/lower-income backgrounds at prestigious institutions treated their place as
  "a big achievement."
- **Donor bias is documented at the institutional level**: Shahid Abdulla, co-founder of the
  **Indus Valley School of Art and Architecture**, noted donors were reluctant to fund an art
  institution, preferring medical, business and engineering schools.
- Some institutions and NGOs do provide financial support to art students.

### Relevance to Learms
This is the **clearest example in the whole Area-2 research of a problem Learms can actually solve
cheaply**, because two of the five named barriers are *information* problems:

1. **"Limited information about art and design courses"** and **"undecided between Fine Arts and
   Design"** — a good career/programme-explorer for arts pathways (what each degree is, what
   graduates do, what it costs, who offers it, what the portfolio requirement is) costs almost
   nothing to build and directly addresses a documented, measured need.
2. **Parental disapproval is the top barrier.** This is the strongest case in the research for a
   **parent-facing artefact**: evidence-based career-outcome information for arts and design, in
   Urdu, that a student can show a parent. *Note the tension with `PROBLEMS_MEDICAL.md` M5 and
   `PROBLEMS_SOCIAL_SCIENCES.md` S5, which say never expose student data to parents — those are
   about **performance and wellness data**. A parent-facing **career-information** page contains no
   student data and is a different thing. Keep the distinction strict.*
3. **Materials cost is the financial bite, not tuition** — so a *scholarships / NGO support /
   materials-funding* directory is more useful to arts students than a discount on Learms.
4. Arts students' need is **portfolio + practice + critique**, which is the hardest thing to deliver
   with AI and the area where Learms should be most humble. Credible contributions: art history and
   theory (text-based, teachable), design fundamentals, software skills (Adobe, Figma, Blender),
   freelancing/business skills, and **written critique practice**. Do **not** claim to replace studio
   critique.

## 2. Precarity and well-being in creative careers (international evidence)

### Topic: What arts graduates' working lives actually look like
### Source: Arnold, S. & O'Brien, A. (2025), "Employment motivations and values in the creative industries: Reorienting from creativity to well-being among Generation Zs in Ireland", *International Journal of Cultural Studies* (SAGE); citing Comunian & England (2020), Deuze (2023), Shaughnessy et al. (2023), Arts Council (2020), Creative Access (2024)
### Key Finding **[MED — Irish sample; transferability to Pakistan unverified]**

- The structural conditions of creative work — **portfolio careers, project-based work, freelancing,
  interning** — "also have a mental and emotional impact for creative workers" [Deuze, 2023].
  Multiple reports find creative workers experience mental-health problems tied to pressured work
  environments [Arts Council 2020; Creative Access 2024].
- **New entrants are the most vulnerable**: "no reputation to lean on, no networks for finding work,
  no track record of work to evidence their abilities" [Comunian & England, 2020].
- Graduates "found little work, were desperate for any work and **accepted poorer conditions**"
  [Pun et al., 2024]; familiar narratives of "poor entry routes, career scrambling, **exploitation
  via unpaid or low-paid internships**."
- **The notable positive finding:** this generation "found job satisfaction in *being mentally well*
  at least as much as *being creative*", prioritising quality of life and work-life balance, and
  associating **creative success with well-being**. Remote/hybrid work is treated as a *reward*.

⚠ **Transferability caveat.** This is an Irish Gen-Z sample in a post-pandemic, high-income labour
market with remote-work norms. The *precarity* mechanisms plausibly generalise to Pakistan; the
*well-being-prioritising response* may not, since it presupposes the option to decline work.
**Do not present Irish findings as Pakistani findings.**

### Relevance to Learms
- **Network and portfolio are the named missing assets for new entrants** — not skills. A portfolio
  builder and a peer/mentor community are therefore higher-leverage for arts students than more
  course content.
- **Freelancing and self-employment literacy** (pricing, contracts, client management, invoicing,
  international payment rails — a real Pakistani pain point) is arguably the single most valuable
  thing Learms could teach an arts or design student. It also connects to Pakistan's large existing
  freelance-services economy.
- The "unpaid internship" pattern is documented in both the Irish study and Pakistani law/media
  (see `PROBLEMS_LAW.md` §4, `PROBLEMS_MEDIA.md` §1): **unpaid early-career work is a cross-field
  Pakistani norm.** Any "career outcomes" messaging must not assume paid entry-level work exists.

### Citation
- https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13675494251317067

## Gaps / Not found
- **No quantitative data at all** on Pakistani arts/humanities student numbers, dropout, or outcomes.
- No Pakistani creative-industries employment or earnings data.
- No Pakistani study of arts-student mental health.
- Humanities proper (literature, history, philosophy, Islamic studies, languages) is **not covered**
  by either source here — both are about *visual/performing arts*. **This remains a genuine hole in
  the Area-2 coverage.** Islamic Studies in particular is a very large enrolment field in Pakistan
  (madrasa + university) and has had **no research in this pass at all**. Flagged to `GAPS.md`.
